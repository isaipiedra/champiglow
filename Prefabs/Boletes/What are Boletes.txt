Boletes in this case are describing a capped mushroom that has pores instead of gills. I attempted to try to make an all encompassing Bolete that could be used for various mushrooms like Porcini (Boletus edulis), or Gyroporus cyanescens (blue bruising Bolete). Some species of Boletes like Gyroporus cyanescens bruise blue when cut open or over ripened. Boletes can be commonly mistaken for Polypores, which also have pores instead of gills, and are more woody than Boletes. Polypores are considered shelf mushrooms though, meaning they’ll have different cap shapes. A common polypore from this pack is Reishi. Some Boletes are edible and some are not. Examples of edible Boletes are Porcini (Boletus edulis) and Gyroporus cyanescens. Examples of inedible Boletes are non-poisonous but intensely bitter Bitter Bolete (Tylopilus felleus), which has pinkish pores, the toxic Devil’s Bolete (red pores and blue bruising) and Boletus huronensis.

Boletes can be found throughout the world and can be found both individually and in clusters. Bolete mushrooms tend to form a mycorrhizal symbiosis with living trees, where the fungal network (mycelium) intertwines with tree roots (though there are some exceptions). These mushrooms require this symbiotic relationship with tree roots to survive, making it very difficult to cultivate these mushrooms. These mushrooms are commonly found growing individually or in clusters on the forest floor next to trees. These mushrooms can be found near both softwood and hardwood trees. Common lookalikes are Polypores and other inedible Boletes.
